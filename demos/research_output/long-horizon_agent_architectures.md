# Long-horizon agent architectures

## Long-Horizon Agent Architectures: A Research Report

The pursuit of artificial general intelligence (AGI) necessitates the development of agents capable of planning and acting effectively over extended time horizons. Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms often struggle with sparse rewards and the credit assignment problem inherent in long-horizon tasks. Recent research has focused on addressing these challenges through novel architectural designs and training methodologies. Hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL), exemplified by systems like the Hierarchical Abstract Machines (HAMs) framework and the FeUdal Networks architecture, aims to decompose complex tasks into simpler sub-goals, enabling agents to learn reusable skills and explore more efficiently. Furthermore, research into intrinsic motivation, as seen in approaches like curiosity-driven exploration and empowerment maximization, seeks to provide agents with internal rewards that encourage exploration and discovery in the absence of external feedback. These methods are often evaluated on challenging benchmarks such as the Atari suite, the DeepMind Lab environment, and custom-designed long-horizon navigation tasks.

Memory-augmented neural networks represent another promising avenue for long-horizon agent architectures. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), particularly LSTMs and GRUs, have demonstrated some success in capturing temporal dependencies, but their limited memory capacity often hinders performance on tasks requiring reasoning over extended periods. Transformer-based architectures, initially developed for natural language processing, have shown remarkable capabilities in capturing long-range dependencies and are increasingly being adapted for RL. The Gato agent, a general-purpose agent trained on a diverse range of tasks, leverages a Transformer architecture to achieve impressive performance across various domains. Furthermore, research into differentiable neural computers (DNCs) and neural Turing machines (NTMs) explores the use of external memory modules to enhance the memory capacity and reasoning abilities of agents. Benchmarks like the bAbI question answering tasks and the CLEVR visual reasoning dataset are commonly used to evaluate the memory and reasoning capabilities of these architectures.
